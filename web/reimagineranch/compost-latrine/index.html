Reimagine Ranch · Build 01

The compost latrine.

Waterless, accessible, and buildable in a community weekend.

A raised, three-stall dry-cover composting latrine with a wheelchair ramp and a covered patio — Reimagine Ranch's first build, and the pilot for the land's community credit-loop. This report is the visual summary of the deeper build research.

01 · The premise

The pitch, in one sentence.

A waterless, three-stall, wheelchair-accessible latrine that turns the simplest possible inputs — a bucket and a scoop of sawdust — into safe compost, on a raised cedar deck the community can frame in a weekend.

Why this is the first build. Any gathering on the land needs sanitation before anything else. It's high-leverage, low-risk, and a perfect teaching project for the community credit-loop to find its feet — while modeling the ranch's values in one small structure: waterless, off-grid, nutrient-cycling, locally sourced, accessible.

02 · The system

Dry-cover composting, three credible forms.

A dry-cover toilet captures waste in a chamber; the user covers each deposit with a carbon material (sawdust, shavings, leaf mould). The cover layer is the whole trick — it blocks odor, balances carbon-to-nitrogen, soaks up moisture, and forms an aerobic biofilter. No water, no chemicals, no electricity. The systems differ in where the composting finishes.

Recommended at launch

Bucket / humanure batch

Bucket under a wooden throne; full buckets cure in an outdoor 3-bin thermophilic pile (130–150 °F kills pathogens). Cheapest, most resilient, needs a committed steward.

Upgrade in place

Twin-vault moldering

Two sealed under-deck vaults, used alternately; each moldering a year before emptying. No daily hauling — the chamber is the composter. The trailhead-privy classic.

Fallback

Commercial composter

Manufactured unit (Clivus / Sun-Mar class). Code-legible and lower-touch, but $1.5k–$9k each, often wants power, less in the DIY/credit-loop spirit.

Build once, choose later. The structure hosts a removable chamber bay under each throne, sized for the taller twin-vault. Operate as a bucket system now; swap buckets for sealed vaults later without rebuilding. Same reversible philosophy as the Airstream Studio's insulation.

03 · Accessibility

The part that sets the geometry.

The accessible stall and its ramp drive most of the building's dimensions, so we fix them first — built to the 2010 ADA Standards on principle and for grant credibility, even though a private community latrine may not be legally bound to them.

The number that sizes the whole footprint. At 1:12, every inch of deck height = one foot of ramp run. So the porch gets two ways up: a gentle 1:12 ramp that climbs onto the porch (the accessible route) and a short stair at the east end for everyone else — cedar plank treads on a low gravel pad with cedar post-and-rail guards (a warmer read than steel pipe), the way a community front porch should feel.

04 · Structure & model

A raised cedar platform, modeled before it's cut.

Raised 24 in so the compost chamber lives underneath and stays removable, the floor stays dry, and gravity helps. Screw-pile footings (no concrete) carry rot-resistant framing, cedar decking, board-and-batten walls that breathe, and a single-slope standing-seam metal roof pitched to the back with a big front overhang shading the patio.

Why Blender first, CAD later

We model the form in Blender to iterate massing, siting, and ramp slope fast — and to produce something the community can actually look at and react to. A precise, board-by-board cut-list model (FreeCAD) comes after the massing and dimensions are approved; that's a fabrication step, not a design step. The scene is generated by a committed Python script, so changing a dimension re-runs into an updated model and render:

blender --background --python rr/blender/compost_latrine.py

05 · Ventilation & materials

Passive stacks, healthy materials.

After the cover material, the vent stack keeps a dry toilet odor-free: one 4-in stack per chamber, straight up through the roof with a rain cap and insect screen. Paint it black and face it south — solar gain warms the stack and drives a convective updraft that pulls air down through the seat and out the roof. A raked-louver vent crown wraps the walls top to bottom for cross-flow, and the whole box breathes without a watt of power.

When still air or a packed gathering needs more, the model offers powered, solar + battery extraction that runs 24/7 — see the vent options below. A roof PV panel and a small battery keep the fans turning day and night, so airflow never stops.

07 · Configurations

One model, every option.

Each choice below renders from a single source file (compost_latrine.py) — pick one from each group and they combine: layout × ventilation × upgrade, with no separate models to maintain.

Bathrooms — 2 or 3

Ventilation — passive, or solar-powered 24/7

The default is fully passive (black stacks + the louver crown). For dead-still air or a packed gathering, any powered option adds a roof PV panel + battery so the fans never stop — day or night.

Upgrade — rainwater catchment + showers

The back-draining metal roof feeds a gutter → cistern → one outdoor shower per stall on the back wall. Bolts on now or later. --upgrade=rainwater

08 · Build paths

Two credible tiers.

Path A · Community weekend

≈ $2.2k–$3.5k materials

Bucket system, screw piles, reclaimed/local lumber, cedar where touched, passive stacks, hand-built grab bars on solid blocking. Labor = community credit-loop. The accessible stall + ramp are non-negotiable even here.

Path B · Permit-ready

≈ $6k–$9k materials

Twin-vault or commercial accessible unit, urine diversion to a mulch basin, standing- seam roof, solar vent fans, finished cedar, code-rated hardware, signage, rain-catch handwash. Survives a health-department visit and a grant site tour.

Recommended: frame the structure to Path B (deck height, vault bays, stack routing) and operate at Path A first. Upgrade in place as credits and crowds accumulate. Build once.

10 · Decisions needed from koH

Open items.

  1. System at launch — bucket (recommended) or twin-vault? Affects steward labor vs build size. Structure supports both either way.
  2. Urine diversion — yes (drier, easier compost, +1 fixture + drain) or no (simpler)? Recommended yes for the accessible stall at minimum.
  3. Layout — 3 bathrooms (16×12 ft) or 2 (12×12 ft)? Drives footprint, cost, and shower count.
  4. Ventilation — passive (default), or a powered solar + battery option (stack fans preferred) for still air and big gatherings?
  5. Rainwater + showers — include the catchment upgrade in the pilot, or phase 2?
  6. Ramp + stair — confirm the 1:12 ramp-onto-porch plus the east stair fits the chosen site.
  7. Build path — confirm "frame to Path B, operate Path A."
  8. Capacity & siting — expected peak users at a gathering, and where on the land (sun angle for the stacks, approach for the ramp).
  9. Permitting — is health-department sign-off in scope? If yes, the commercial-unit fallback and urine handling gain weight.

Once the system, ramp geometry, and tier are settled, the next step is a fabrication-grade cut-list model and a material takeoff — the point at which Blender massing hands off to CAD.
