01 · The premise

The pitch, in one sentence.

Turn a gutted '67 Airstream Overlander into a four-mode podcast and video production studio that lives on Mystic Hearts land, is bookable through the Mystic Hearts scheduling system by tenants (in credits) and outside creators (in fiat), and whose construction itself capitalizes the Mystic Hearts credit pool through volunteer-earned credits.

The build doubles as the proof-of-concept for HAND's pool-of-resources model. People earn credits doing real work on a real asset, then redeem those credits for studio time, practitioner sessions, or event access. The studio is not just paid for in credits, its existence is what gives the credit pool somewhere meaningful to flow.

Why this is more than a vanity project

Most regenerative-economy proofs are theoretical or transactional. This one is physical, public, durable, and earns its keep. A renter sees a tangible asset built by people who earned credits doing it. That's a different kind of legitimacy than a spreadsheet.

04 · Vehicle

The '67 Overlander, gutted to the shell.

A 1967 Airstream Overlander Land Yacht at 27 ft is a sweet-spot vintage. Long enough for credible panel seating with a B-roll camera; short enough to maneuver on the land. The trade is that it's a 59-year-old aluminum shell, and the only honest path is a full restoration before any studio fit-out begins. The good news: the unit is already gutted, which means nothing has to be undone first. Everything can be built right.

Phase 0.5 scope, in rough sequence

  1. Frame inspection and repair. Check for rust at outriggers, subfloor mating points, axle attachments. Weld as needed.
  2. Subfloor. New 5/8 or 3/4-inch marine-grade plywood, sealed, bonded to frame.
  3. Insulation. Layered cork-and-wool stack with aerogel rib breaks and a smart vapor retarder. Critical for thermal comfort, acoustic isolation, and humidity buffering. The Airstream's curved aluminum skin is a tuning fork without it. See §5 for the full layer library and two build paths.
  4. Wiring. Full new 12V DC plus 120V AC, modern panel, GFCI/AFCI where code requires, RV-rated.
  5. Plumbing. Only if the unit will have a bathroom or galley sink. Studio-use case may not need it. Open question.
  6. Windows. Original single-pane Airstream windows are acoustic and thermal sieves. Three options: replace with dual-pane, retain originals plus add removable interior secondary glazing for session use, or reduce window count entirely.
  7. Skin and seals. Inspect rivets, replace any compromised; reseal all seams and windows.
  8. HVAC install (Phase 0.6, see next section).
  9. Interior fit-out, done with acoustics in mind: cavities filled per the §5 stack (cork on flat sections, sheep wool in the curves, rockwool reserved for priority acoustic zones) before final paneling, so finished surfaces work with the acoustic plan rather than against it.

Sourcing the restoration labor

DIY by Aiylah, Takata, and the volunteer pool, paid in Mystic Hearts credits via the ledger, works for: demo, insulation, paneling, flooring, simple wiring runs under licensed supervision, interior finish carpentry, painting, sealing, mounting. Hired specialists are needed for: structural frame welding, full electrical permit work, HVAC line-set install (unless the MRCOOL DIY mini-split is chosen). Vintage Airstream restoration shops exist nationally (Timeless Travel Trailers, Frank's Trailer Works) but at $150 to $250+ per hour are usually overkill. Local welder plus licensed electrician plus HVAC tech is cheaper per-hour and probably the right path.

Rough Phase 0.5 budget

$8k (low) to $25k (high), depending on DIY-vs-hired ratio and window strategy.

  • Low end assumes DIY everything except the licensed electrical hookup, retain original windows with secondary glazing, MRCOOL DIY for HVAC.
  • High end assumes hired electrical, hired HVAC, dual-pane window replacement, outside frame welding.
05 · Insulation

Layered, healthy, reversible.

The wall assembly does four jobs at once: thermal isolation, sound out, sound in, and humidity buffering for two people breathing in a sealed aluminum tube. Closed-cell spray foam is the industry default for fast van builds, but it bulges vintage Airstream skins, off-gasses, and is unrepairable. For this trailer we want healthy, breathable, repairable. Cork and sheep wool are the headline materials.

Wall stack · outside (left) to inside (right)
Expanded cork (Thermacork / Amorim ICB) Sheep wool (Havelock) Aerogel rib break (Aspen Spaceloft) Decoupling, pro tier only

Cavity depth is ~1.5 to 2.5 in between skin and inner ribs. Aerogel goes on the steel ribs only (the thermal bridges that bypass the cavity fill). Cork is rigid and goes in flat sections; wool conforms to the curved roof crown and end caps. The smart vapor retarder (e.g., CertainTeed MemBrain) sits warm-side of the cavity to catch winter condensation drive, then opens up in summer to release stored moisture. Pro-tier layers add decoupling, mass, and Green Glue damping in front of the standard stack.

A. Indie build

Volunteer-installable

DIY-friendly

Cork on flat sections, sheep wool in the curves, aerogel rib breaks, smart vapor retarder, plywood finish with acoustic panels in priority zones. Every layer reversible. Healthy materials, low off-gassing, repairable by anyone with hand tools.

Wall R-value R-9 to R-11 Materials cost ~$2,500 Labor 80 to 140 volunteer hours, paid in Mystic Hearts credits Acoustic ceiling Well-recorded podcast tier

B. Blended path

Recommended

Build indie, leave room

Build the indie stack now, but commit upfront to the aerogel rib breaks and a continuous smart vapor retarder. Those are the only layers that live inside the cavity and can't be retrofitted without opening the wall. Pro decoupling (RSIC clips, MLV, Green Glue) can bolt on later if the studio's revenue justifies it.

Phase 0.5 spend ~$2,500 materials Optional upgrade later +~$3,800 mat'ls + labor for full pro decoupling Decision deferred Until first REW noise-floor measurement

C. Professional build

Commercial-grade quiet

Specialist install

Indie stack plus a full decoupling layer in front: RSIC clips, hat channel, mass-loaded vinyl, Green Glue between two layers of plywood, acoustician-tuned absorber face, optional floating floor on U-boats. STC mid-50s achievable in a 27 ft trailer.

Wall R-value R-13 to R-16 Materials cost ~$6,900 Specialist labor $15k to $25k + $1.5k to $3k acoustician When justified Paid commercial recording for outside clients

Recommendation: Option B, blended path

Build the indie stack now. Track the noise floor with a REW measurement after HVAC install (Phase 0.6) and the first acoustic treatment pass. Decide later whether to add the pro decoupling layer. The one decision that has to happen at the indie stage: aerogel rib thermal breaks on every steel rib (~$400 to $800). Those are inside the cavity; adding them later means opening the wall. Everything else can wait.

Materials to avoid in any path: closed-cell spray polyurethane foam (bulges vintage Airstream skins from the inside, off-gasses for months, turns to powder in cavities over decades, and adheres permanently so any future skin or electrical work is destructive); plain 6-mil polyethylene vapor barrier (wrong half the year in a mobile vehicle that sees every weather condition); fiberglass batts (particulate, itch, mediocre moisture handling).

Full layer library, vendor sourcing, and sources: funding/framing/airstream-insulation-research.md

06 · HVAC

The single highest-leverage spend in the entire build.

HVAC noise floor sets the ceiling of how good the studio's audio can ever be. Once installed, it's not cheap to swap. This decision matters more than the choice of microphones.

Operating noise floor · lower is better
Below mic noise floor Above mic noise floor Audibility threshold (~30 dB)

Reference: whisper ≈ 30 dB · quiet office ≈ 50 dB · normal conversation ≈ 60 dB · dB SPL is logarithmic; +10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud.

A. Mini-split heat pump

Recommended

Quietest practical

Near-silent indoor head. Compressor mounted outside the trailer. Heats and cools. Lets the studio record with HVAC running, which is the whole point.

Examples MRCOOL DIY 12k, Mitsubishi Mr. Slim 9k Install MRCOOL is DIY; Mitsubishi needs a tech Rough cost $2,000 to $4,000 with install

B. Quiet rooftop unit

Budget path

Workable

Better than legacy roof units but still audible on any mic. Self-contained, familiar install path. Workflow becomes "AC off while rolling."

Examples Dometic Penguin II, Furrion Chill Rough cost $800 to $1,500 with install Trade-off Always audible during recording

C. Ducted hybrid

Probably overkill

Premium

Mini-split paired with low-CFM ducting. Studio-grade silence. Even temperature distribution. Most expensive and complex install.

Rough cost $4,000 to $7,000+ When justified Commercial clients only

Recommendation: Option A, mini-split

The cost delta over a rooftop unit, roughly $1,500 to $2,500, is the highest-leverage dollar in the build for audio quality. Worth pulling from gear budget if needed. MRCOOL DIY 12k is the value pick if Aiylah or Takata have install capacity. Mitsubishi Mr. Slim 9k is the best-in-class quiet pick if hired install is in scope.

09 · Operator model

Who runs the room.

Three options, with recommendation. Aiylah and Takata on the land plus the volunteer pool make Option C realistically viable.

A. Fully self-serve

Renter runs it alone

Cheapest to run

Renter shows up, follows a setup template, runs the session, leaves. Highest idiot-proofing bar. Gear damage and misuse risk highest. Bad sessions hurt the brand.

B. On-site operator only

Producer included

Highest quality

A trained operator runs every session. Highest output quality, lowest gear-damage risk. Operator can upsell editing or production. Adds labor cost on every booking.

C. Tiered, self-serve + add-on

Recommended

Best of both

Renters choose at booking: solo (cheaper) or with producer (more expensive). Nervous renters self-select into the operator path. Template and idiot-proofing investment becomes high-leverage.

Operator pool is already named

Aiylah and Takata are on the land and could be trained as operators. Open question whether either of them wants the role; it's real work and not everyone wants to be the gear person. The volunteer plus small-pay pool may also produce a third candidate. Direct conversation with Aiylah and Takata is the right next step, not assumption.

10 · Platform

Mystic Hearts platform integration.

The studio needs to show up in the Mystic Hearts scheduling system as a bookable resource alongside practitioner sessions. Some of this lifts existing MH platform work; some adds new requirements.

Concrete platform needs

  • Bookable resource type, sibling to Practitioner. Probably worth modeling as a generic BookableResource entity so other shared assets can be added later (vans, gear, gathering spaces).
  • Multi-hour booking blocks, recommend 4-hour minimum (vs. the 1-hour blocks practitioner sessions use).
  • Dual pricing surface, credits plus fiat, if Option A booking is selected.
  • Add-on at booking, operator add-on if Option C operator model is selected.
  • Pre-arrival info packet, auto-sent on confirmation: parking, door code, WiFi, contact, house rules, waiver link.
  • Damage deposit or waiver, required at booking. Stripe pre-auth hold or upfront.
  • Post-session feedback, reuse MH's verified-attendee review system if it generalizes to non-practitioner resources.
  • Credit-to-fiat pricing rule, only if Option A; coordinate with the broader Mystic Hearts brief. Needed regardless once cross-Reciprocate redemption activates.

Architectural question for the MH side

Is the studio modeled as an extension of the existing practitioner-booking system (one calendar engine, two listing types) or as a separate resource-booking module? Recommend extending the existing engine: same time-slot logic, same payment integration, just a different listing surface.

12 · Strategic

The build-labor credit loop.

The studio doesn't just accept the Mystic Hearts credit economy as payment. Its construction capitalizes the credit pool that it will later accept. This is the cleanest possible proof of HAND's pool-of-resources model.

Volunteers (orange nodes) earn credits doing build labor. The MH ledger holds them. Tenants spend them on studio sessions. The studio runs, generates value, and the loop continues. Outside renters (teal) feed fiat into the studio in parallel, funding operations and gear upgrades.

Dependency

Requires that the MH credit ledger and the bounty board (both in MH v1) are live before build-labor credit issuance starts, or that hours are logged manually now and back-credited when the ledger goes live. The second option is fine and probably necessary, since restoration starts before MH v1 ships. A simple shared spreadsheet of "who did what hours when" is sufficient until the ledger is real.

Two-tier credit rates

The Mystic Hearts brief defines a general rate (1 hour = 1 credit) and a vetted-specialist rate (higher, per-task admin judgment). The Airstream restoration is the first real use case for the specialist tier: licensed electrical, HVAC install, frame welding, finish carpentry all qualify. General hours (demo, insulation, painting, mounting, cabling) earn the general rate. This makes the studio build the proving ground for the specialist tier as well as the general tier.

15 · Procurement

Immediate buys.

Two items pre-greenlit ahead of the broader Tier 1 spend, because they're useful before the trailer is even on the pad and carry forward into every tier unchanged. Queue them up now.

RØDE Rodecaster Pro II

Mixer + recorder + sound pads, one box

~$599–699

The unambiguous best podcast production console at this price. Four XLR/TRS combo inputs with Revolution preamps (clean enough to run an SM7B without a Cloudlifter), built-in SD-card recorder, USB-C audio interface to two computers simultaneously, Bluetooth caller, eight programmable sound pads, multi-track export. Carries forward unchanged from Tier 1 through Tier 2.

Best source Sweetwater (free 2-yr warranty ext.) Alt. sources B&H Photo, RØDE direct Buy as Unit only — no bundle needed yet Carries forward Tier 1 → Tier 2 unchanged

Desview T3 teleprompter

HDMI beam-splitter, any camera behind

~$280–350

Beam-splitter style: the camera mounts behind a 70/30 glass at 45°, the presenter reads text reflected on the glass, the camera shoots cleanly through. Built-in HDMI monitor means no iPad-app lock-in — drive it from any laptop, tablet, or phone with an HDMI dongle. Wide opening accommodates anything from a ZV-E10 to an FX30 with a chunky lens. The "any camera behind" pick that future-proofs against the Tier 2 multi-camera upgrade.

Best source B&H Photo or Amazon Camera fit Lens diameter up to ~110mm Display Built-in HDMI monitor (driven by laptop / tablet via HDMI) Carries forward All tiers

If the Desview T3 isn't right, here's the field at a glance. All are beam-splitter style (camera shoots through the glass).

iPad / phone driven

Most popular compact teleprompter among podcasters. Mounts your iPad or phone directly. Smaller cameras only (mirrorless, fits up to ~80mm lens). The "I already have an iPad" pick.

The OG broadcast teleprompter brand. Heavier, more durable, fits cinema cameras and chunky lenses. Probably overkill for v1 but the right answer if the studio ever does client production work.

Why these two now

Both are useful immediately, before the trailer is on the pad. Rodecaster Pro II can record interviews, sound check existing mics, and start producing content the day it arrives. The teleprompter is a tenant + creator amenity that pays for itself fast — the kind of small thing that signals "this studio is set up by someone who cares." Both survive every tier decision unchanged, so no risk of buying twice.

Price reality check

All prices are rough 2026 estimates. Verify at the vendor before ordering — RØDE prices have been stable but accessory pricing drifts, and teleprompter prices vary 10–20% between resellers. Sweetwater's price-match is usually worth one phone call.
