# Permaculture & Low-Tech Build Methods — Build Research

*Reimagine Ranch · cross-build method study · research compiled 2026-05-30*

Companion research that sits **upstream of the individual build docs**. Where
[`compost-latrine.md`](compost-latrine.md) decides *what* RR's first structure is, this doc decides
*how we build the small structures* — which wall and frame methods are cheap, fast, low-tool, and
honest to the land. It feeds two near-term builds: the **louvered screen walls** (TODO: NEXT UP) and
the **"day-build" bathroom** (TODO: LATER) — and re-checks the latrine's own framing choices.

It mirrors the depth of the latrine and Airstream research. Read this before any conversation about
*how* a wall, frame, or panel on RR gets made.

---

## 0. The frame, in one sentence

> RR builds small (sub-200 sq ft), off-grid, accessible structures with a tiny rotating crew and
> mostly local/salvaged material — so the method has to be **cheap, fast, low-tool, repairable, and
> survive Central Texas sun-and-downpour** before it earns a single fundable adjective.

Central Texas (Austin / Hill Country) is the climate that picks winners here: **hot humid summers,
intense UV, hard wind-driven rain, then dry spells** — a wet-and-dry cycle that is unkind to anything
that stays damp or that bakes in the sun. The two protective truths repeat across *every* natural
method below and are worth stating once: **a good roof with a wide overhang ("a good hat") and a
raised, dry foundation ("good boots")** are what make any of these last. Get those two right and the
wall material almost doesn't matter; get them wrong and even the best wall rots.

---

## 1. What RR is actually choosing between

RR's structures are small and have **three different jobs**, and the right method differs by job:

1. **Frame / load path** — what holds the roof up (the latrine deck, posts, the day-build shell).
2. **Wall infill / screen** — what fills between the frame (privacy, shade, weather, the louvered
   screen walls).
3. **Speed tier** — full build (a weekend+, e.g. the latrine) vs. **day-build** (one day, minimal
   tools, rapid-deploy bathroom).

Most "natural building" methods (cob, earthbag, strawbale, cordwood) are **wall systems**, not frames
or fast systems. That distinction is the whole analysis: a thermal-mass earth wall and a one-day
bathroom are answering different questions.

## 2. The methods, assessed for RR

Each is scored against RR's real constraints: cost, tools/skill, build speed (can a small crew do it
in a day or a weekend?), durability in Central Texas heat/sun/rain, permaculture fit, accessibility,
and code/permit reality. Only claims the method genuinely earns.

### 2a. Cob (clay + sand + straw, monolithic)
- **What:** Hand-sculpted earth walls, often from on-site soil. No forms, no machinery.
- **Cost:** Lowest possible — soil is often free, sometimes dug from the build site itself.
- **Tools/skill:** Almost none in tools (feet, hands, tarps); **high in time and body-labor**, low
  in transferable "skill." Forgiving and teachable.
- **Speed:** **Slow.** Cob is laid in lifts that must firm up before the next; a wall is weeks of
  curing, not a weekend. **Not a day-build method.**
- **Durability (Central TX):** Excellent *if* protected — properly roofed, foundationed, and
  lime-plastered cob lasts centuries (500+ yr English cob homes). Cob itself is **not waterproof**;
  it needs the wide overhang + raised foundation + lime plaster against wind-driven rain. Thermal
  mass shines where nights cool off — Austin summer nights stay warm, so mass is a *modest* not
  magic cooling win.
- **Permaculture fit:** Highest of any method — local soil, zero manufactured embodied carbon,
  fully compostable back to earth.
- **Accessibility:** Heavy walls don't conflict with an accessible *layout*; the build labor is
  physically demanding (a barrier to who can build it).
- **Code/permit:** Earthen building is a permitting gray area; fine for a sub-200 sq ft unpermitted
  accessory structure, harder if engineering sign-off is ever needed.
- **RR verdict:** **Showcase method, not a workhorse.** Beautiful for a future bench, a thermal-mass
  feature wall, or a heat-sink element — but too slow for the latrine or day-build.

### 2b. Earthbag / Superadobe (earth-filled bags, coursed)
- **What:** Polypropylene tubes/bags filled with site soil, tamped, stacked, barbed-wire between
  courses, plastered. CalEarth's SuperAdobe is the codified version.
- **Cost:** Low — main material is local soil; **hyperadobe (mesh tube) ≈ half the cost of
  superadobe**. Bags/wire/plaster are the only bought items.
- **Tools/skill:** Minimal tools (bags, tamper, shovels, buckets); low skill, very teachable —
  CalEarth's pitch is "anyone, anywhere, manual labor." Good crew-teaching method.
- **Speed:** **Faster than cob** (coursed, predictable rhythm) but still **not a day-build** for an
  enclosed room — filling, tamping, and plastering a structure is multi-day to multi-week.
- **Durability (Central TX):** Strong — earthbag walls last decades to a century with proper
  foundation, waterproofing, overhang, and breathable plaster; documented resistance to quake,
  hurricane, fire. UV degrades exposed polypropylene bags fast, so **they must be plastered
  promptly** — a real Texas-sun caveat.
- **Permaculture fit:** High — site soil, low embodied carbon (bags + a little stabilizer the
  exception). Domes/curves reduce roof material.
- **Accessibility:** Layout-neutral; curved earthbag rooms can complicate a 60-in turn circle and
  out-swing door geometry the latrine needs — plan deliberately.
- **Code/permit:** **Best code story of the earth methods** — *cement-stabilized* SuperAdobe is
  recognized under the 2018 IRC Appendix and 2019 CA Building Code; acceptance still varies by
  jurisdiction.
- **RR verdict:** **Strong candidate for a permanent, low-roofed structure** (a root cellar,
  pump house, thermal-mass utility room) where mass and an earth-sheltered look are wanted. Overkill
  and too slow for the day-build bathroom.

### 2c. Cordwood masonry (log-ends + mortar)
- **What:** Short seasoned log-ends laid like bricks in a mortar/cob matrix, often within a
  post-and-beam frame.
- **Cost:** Low if logs are salvaged/on-site; the finished wall is **inside-and-out done in one
  pass** (no separate sheathing/siding/insulation/drywall) — a real cost saving.
- **Tools/skill:** Modest — anyone can lay it, but **wood seasoning is the gotcha**: green logs
  shrink in the mortar and crack, killing R-value and needing re-pointing. Skill is in wood prep,
  not laying.
- **Speed:** Masonry rhythm, **faster than cob per wall area** but still **multi-day**; the
  long-pole item is *seasoning the logs a year ahead* — not a spontaneous build.
- **Durability (Central TX):** Mixed and **detail-dependent.** Proponents report cordwood walls stay
  dry and breathe out a soaking; detractors report rot, shrinkage, and insect damage. In humid
  Central Texas the breathability cuts both ways — **needs the wide-overhang "good hat" and periodic
  mortar maintenance.**
- **Permaculture fit:** High — uses salvage/standing-dead wood, closes a local wood loop, low
  embodied energy.
- **Accessibility:** Layout-neutral.
- **Code/permit:** Gray area like the other natural-wall methods; fine for small accessory scale.
- **RR verdict:** **Niche / character build.** Lovely for a feature wall using ranch deadwood, but
  the year-ahead seasoning requirement and Texas-humidity rot risk keep it off the critical path.

### 2d. Strawbale (baled straw infill, plastered)
- **What:** Straw bales stacked as insulating infill (usually in a post-and-beam frame:
  "Nebraska-style" load-bearing is the exception), plastered both sides.
- **Cost:** Bales are cheap, **but bales are only ~15% of the budget** — foundation, frame, roof,
  and (critically) plaster labor dominate. Texas-built cost data lands near conventional
  ($130–150+/sq ft) once everything is counted.
- **Tools/skill:** Low for stacking; **the plaster is the craft and the schedule**.
- **Speed:** **Slow overall** — whole-house strawbale runs 4–10 months; even a small room is
  weeks once plastered. **Not a day-build.**
- **Durability (Central TX):** Workable — **Texas is an active strawbale region** and bales perform
  if installed dry (≤14% MC), kept under 20% MC long-term, and sealed in **breathable lime or earth
  plaster (never cement in humid climates)**. A century is achievable with good detailing. The
  failure mode is trapped moisture — the one thing Central Texas humidity tests hardest.
- **Permaculture fit:** High — agricultural byproduct, excellent insulation, low embodied energy.
- **Accessibility:** Thick walls reduce interior clear width — must be sized in for the accessible
  stall's 60-in circle; otherwise layout-neutral.
- **RR verdict:** **Best when insulation is the point** — i.e. the *conditioned, all-weather* comfort
  tiers in the TODO (Tier 2/3 envelope), not a breezy latrine or a one-day bathroom.

### 2e. Bamboo (poles, frame and/or screen)
- **What:** Bamboo culms as structural frame, screen, or both.
- **Cost:** Low where bamboo grows; in Texas it's a **bought/regional material** (Moso is now grown
  commercially in the SE US) — not a free on-site loop unless RR plants a stand (a multi-year play).
- **Tools/skill:** Moderate — bamboo joinery (lashing, fish-mouth cuts, pinned/bolted joints) is a
  distinct skill; treatment is a process step.
- **Speed:** A pre-cut, pre-treated bamboo screen or light frame **can go up fast** (lashed/bolted) —
  the closest of the "natural" materials to a day-build *for screens and light shade structures*.
- **Durability (Central TX):** **The weak point.** Untreated bamboo lasts only **2–3 years**;
  **borax/boric-acid treatment + "durability by design" (elevated, dry, big overhangs, drip details)
  gets it to 30–40 years.** Boron **washes out if it gets wet**, so exposed-to-rain bamboo is a
  maintenance commitment. UV also checks and splits it.
- **Permaculture fit:** High *if* grown/sourced regionally; the borax treatment is benign.
- **Accessibility:** Layout-neutral as frame/screen.
- **RR verdict:** **Good for screens and shade (louvered walls, trellises, the cooling-tier living
  shade), with a treatment + maintenance commitment.** Not the first choice for a weather-tight
  structural shell in this climate.

### 2f. Light salvaged-timber post-and-beam / pole frame
- **What:** A small number of stout posts (set on piers/screw piles or in a gravel pad) carry beams
  and the roof; walls hang off the frame as infill or screen. This is **what the latrine deck
  already is**, and it's the universal *frame* under most natural-wall methods.
- **Cost:** Low-moderate; very low with salvaged/on-site timber. A small post-frame's material is
  mostly posts + beams + metal roof.
- **Tools/skill:** Standard carpentry hand/power tools; widely known skill, easy to teach a crew.
  Most transferable skill on this list.
- **Speed:** **Fast for the frame** — a small post-and-beam shell can stand in a day; a full enclosed
  pole structure is a **handful of weekends** (a 12×16 post-frame ≈ 4–6 weekends). Setting posts
  (and curing concrete, if used) is the long pole — **RR's screw-pile / gravel-pad detail removes the
  concrete cure** and keeps it fast and reversible.
- **Durability (Central TX):** **Excellent and proven** — it's the regional vernacular. Rot-resistant
  cedar/cypress where it touches ground or hands, metal roof, raised off soil. Already the latrine's
  spec.
- **Permaculture fit:** High with local/salvaged timber + screw piles (no concrete) + metal roof
  (rain-harvest-ready). Lower if buying new dimensional lumber.
- **Accessibility:** **Best on this list** — an open frame lets you place doors, clear floor, turn
  circles, and ramps freely. The latrine's ADA geometry depends on this freedom.
- **Code/permit:** The most code-legible method here — ordinary framing inspectors understand it.
- **RR verdict:** **The workhorse frame for RR.** Already chosen for the latrine; it's the right
  skeleton for the louvered walls and the day-build shell too.

### 2g. Pallet / reclaimed-frame
- **What:** Standardized shipping pallets (or other salvaged framing) used as ready-made wall panels
  or a stud-frame substitute.
- **Cost:** Near-zero materials (free/cheap pallets).
- **Tools/skill:** Low — deconstruct, screw together; very teachable.
- **Speed:** **Genuinely fast** — pallets are pre-built panels, so a small pallet-frame shell can go
  up in **a day**. One of the few true day-build candidates.
- **Durability (Central TX):** **The catch.** Pallets are thin, softwood, and weather poorly raw —
  **untreated/unknown pallets deteriorate fast and may carry contamination.** Use **only HT
  (heat-treated, stamped "HT") pallets — never MB (methyl-bromide) treated**, which are toxic. Even
  HT pallets need to be **kept dry under a real roof and off the ground** to last.
- **Permaculture fit:** Strong *reuse/salvage* story; weak as a long-life material. Best framed as
  diverting waste from landfill.
- **Accessibility:** Pallet module sizes are clumsy for precise ADA openings — workable but fiddly.
- **Code/permit:** Fine at unpermitted accessory scale; would not pass a formal structural review as
  a primary wall system.
- **RR verdict:** **Excellent for the day-build / prototype tier and for non-critical screens**
  (compost-bin surrounds, the screen behind the latrine, a temporary shell) — explicitly *HT-only,
  under a good hat.* Not for a structure meant to last decades.

### 2h. Rapid-deploy framed / SIP-style panels
- **What:** Pre-fabricated panels — either shop-built light timber-framed panels or true **SIPs**
  (foam core between OSB skins) — cut and numbered off-site, then assembled on-site like a kit.
- **Cost:** Highest up-front of the lot (manufactured panels / OSB / foam), lowest in *on-site
  labor-hours*. SIP foam/OSB is the least "natural" material here.
- **Tools/skill:** Low on-site skill — panels are pre-cut and numbered; assembly is screws and a
  small crew, no machinery.
- **Speed:** **Fastest enclosure on the list** — SIP/panel builds dry-in **~50% faster than stick
  framing**; a small shell can be **under roof in a day** by hand. **The premier true day-build
  method for an enclosed, insulated room.**
- **Durability (Central TX):** Good if detailed — but OSB-skinned SIPs are **moisture-intolerant**;
  any leak that wets the core is a serious failure, so flashing/overhang discipline is non-negotiable
  in a wet climate. A *shop-built local-timber* panel (instead of SIP) trades some speed for far
  better repairability and a better material story.
- **Permaculture fit:** **Weakest** if true SIPs (foam, glue, OSB embodied energy). **Recoverable**
  if RR makes its *own* panels from local/salvaged timber + natural insulation — same kit-of-parts
  speed, honest materials.
- **Accessibility:** Excellent — panels are designed to a plan, so ADA openings/clearances are
  built in.
- **Code/permit:** SIPs are well-documented and code-recognized; RR-built panels live at accessory
  scale.
- **RR verdict:** **The model for the day-build bathroom — but built RR's way:** pre-fab the panels
  in advance from local/salvaged timber on a post-and-beam/skid base, then assemble in a day. Buy
  true SIPs only if a funded, conditioned unit ever needs maximum speed + insulation.

for houses.

## 4. Which methods are genuinely "buildable in a day"

A small crew, in one day, with minimal/no power tools — honestly assessed:

- **Yes, an enclosed room in a day:** **Rapid-deploy panels** (pre-fab the panels *ahead*, assemble
  in a day) and **pallet/reclaimed shells** (HT pallets, under a roof). Both depend on the *frame and
  panels being prepared in advance* — the "day" is assembly day.
- **Yes, the frame/skeleton in a day:** **Light post-and-beam / pole frame** — posts + beams + a
  metal roof can stand in a day on screw piles or a gravel pad; walls/screens follow.
- **Yes, but only screens / shade:** **Bamboo** (lashed/bolted, pre-treated) and **light louvered
  timber panels** — fast because they aren't weather-tight rooms.
- **No (weeks+):** **Cob, earthbag, cordwood, strawbale.** These are mass/infill wall systems whose
  curing, seasoning, or plastering schedule rules out a one-day enclosed build. They are *permanent*
  methods, not *rapid* ones.

The honest pattern: **a day-build is won in the prep, not the day.** Anything truly assemblable in a
day was largely built (cut, treated, panelized) beforehand. The earth methods can't cheat their cure
time, so they're never day-builds.

## 5. Recommendations

### (a) Latrine + louvered screen walls — *keep and extend the post-and-beam workhorse*
The latrine's **light salvaged-timber post-and-beam on screw piles, metal roof, cedar where touched**
([`compost-latrine.md` §3](compost-latrine.md)) is already the right call — it scores best on
durability, accessibility, code legibility, and speed-of-frame, and its screw-pile/no-concrete and
metal-roof choices carry the permaculture story honestly. **No change recommended.**

For the **louvered screen walls** (TODO NEXT UP — the framed horizontal-louver cedar panel): build
them as **light post-and-rail timber frames with clear-cedar louver blades** — the same family as the
frame, fast to shop-build, and durable in sun/rain because cedar is rot/UV-tolerant and a louver
*sheds* water by design. **Bamboo is a viable alternative blade material for a lower-cost or more
organic screen**, accepting the borax-treatment + maintenance commitment. Avoid raw pallet wood for
any screen that faces weather unbacked.

### (b) Day-build bathroom — *RR-built rapid-deploy panels on a light frame*
**Top recommendation: a pre-fabricated, panelized build on a light post-and-beam / skid base —
assembled in a day, but with panels and frame built ahead from local/salvaged (HT-only) timber.**

Rationale, against RR's constraints:
- It's the **only honest way to get an enclosed, accessible room up in a single day** — the prep is
  done in advance, the day is assembly. (SIP/panel enclosures dry-in ~50% faster than stick framing
  and need no machinery.)
- It **keeps the permaculture story** *if RR builds its own panels* from local/salvaged timber + a
  natural insulation/cover rather than buying foam SIPs — same kit-of-parts speed, honest materials.
- It **inherits the latrine's strengths**: same post-and-beam/screw-pile/metal-roof "hat and boots,"
  so it survives Central Texas sun and downpour; same open-frame freedom to build in the accessible
  clearances.
- The **dry-cover (sawdust/bucket) sanitation core** from the latrine research drops straight in —
  the day-build is a *rapid-deploy sibling*, not a new system.

**Fallback / prototype tier:** an **HT-pallet + post-frame shell** for a first throwaway prototype or
a genuinely budget event unit — fastest and cheapest, explicitly *HT-stamped pallets only, kept dry
under a good roof, not expected to last decades.* Use it to prove the layout, then build the
keeper in panels.

Across both: **the roof goes on first conceptually** (design the overhang and the raised base before
anything else) — the one rule every method on this list shares.
