# Airstream Studio: Insulation Research and Build Recommendations

*Companion research for `web/mystichearts/airstreamstudio/index.html`. Two complete build paths (indie and professional), built up from a full library of viable layers. Voice and constraint context come from the Airstream Studio brief. Read both together.*

---

## Why insulation is the highest-leverage envelope decision

The Airstream Studio brief already names HVAC as the highest-leverage spend because HVAC noise floor caps how good the room can ever sound. **Insulation is the second-highest-leverage spend, and arguably tied for first**, because the wall and floor assembly does four jobs at once:

1. **Thermal isolation.** Aluminum is a thermal sieve; an uninsulated Airstream is unusable in summer or winter.
2. **Acoustic isolation (sound transmission out and in).** The curved aluminum skin is a tuning fork. Mass and decoupling stop external noise getting in and recorded audio leaking out.
3. **Acoustic absorption (room sound).** What's inside the cavity affects how the finished room behaves at the mic.
4. **Moisture management.** Two people breathing in a sealed aluminum tube produce a pint of water vapor per hour. Without the right vapor strategy, that water finds the cold aluminum skin, condenses, and rots whatever organic material is touching it.

Getting this wrong is expensive in three ways: the build fails acoustically, the trailer rots from inside, or the insulation has to be ripped out for any future electrical or skin work. The closed-cell spray foam path the brief currently lists is fastest for crews who do it daily, but it locks in a non-reversible assembly that **has a documented history of bulging vintage Airstream skins** from the inside out and turning to powder over decades. Both build paths below assume we want something we can open back up.

---

## Constraints unique to this project

Restating from the Airstream Studio brief and 1967 Overlander reality:

- **Curved aluminum skin, no thermal break to the steel ribs.** Heat conducts through any rib that touches both sides. Rib-to-skin thermal breaks are a separate small project.
- **Weep holes in the C-channel** at the floor. The condensation path must terminate there, not be sealed off by insulation. This is non-negotiable.
- **27 ft of cavity, mostly 1.5 in to 2.5 in deep** between skin and inner ribs. Some cavities deeper at the belly and roof crown. Cavity depth caps your max R-value per inch material choice.
- **Mobile.** Every layer adds weight, and weight reduces fuel economy, towing safety, and tire/axle load margin. Target finished envelope: under ~600 lb of insulation total.
- **Healing-adjacent end use.** Off-gassing matters more here than in a typical RV build. The Mystic Hearts collective should not be downstream of a 27 ft tube of curing isocyanates.
- **DIY-friendly install paid in Mystic Hearts credits.** Layers that need licensed installers or specialty PPE narrow the volunteer pool.
- **Recording studio acoustics.** This is not "comfortable to sleep in." This is "the noise floor of the room is measured in dB and we care about ±2 dB."

These constraints together rule out a few common van-build defaults: closed-cell spray foam (off-gassing, irreversibility, skin damage risk), fiberglass batts (itch, vapor handling, particulate), reflective bubble wrap as a primary layer (low real R-value when compressed, marketing-driven claims).

## The full layer library

Read this section before either build path. Both builds are made from these layers; the differences are which layers, in what order, and who installs them. Costs are USA, mid-2026, freight not included.

### A. Cavity-fill thermal layers (the bulk insulator)

These are the materials that actually deliver R-value. One of them is the primary cavity fill.

#### A1. Expanded cork board (ICB / Thermacork)
**What it is.** Pure cork bark, granulated, steamed at ~300°C in an autoclave so the cork's own suberin binds it together. No added glue, no binder, no flame retardant. Rigid board, dark brown, smells faintly of campfire.

**Performance.**
- R-value: 3.6 to 4.0 per inch (Thermacork lists R-8 for 2 in, R-12 for 3 in)
- Vapor permeance: ~2.2 perms at 40 mm. Vapor-open, hygroscopic, buffers humidity without losing R.
- Acoustic: excellent broadband absorption, particularly mid and high. Used as the absorber face in studio rooms.
- Fire: Class E (Euro). Resists burn-through for over an hour at 40 mm. No flame retardants needed.
- Off-gassing: passes France's stringent VOC suite. Zero impact on indoor air quality. The "smoky" smell is suberin and dissipates.
- Pest resistance: cork is naturally resistant to insects and rodents.
- Longevity: thermal performance does not degrade for 50+ years. Foam loses R-value over decades; cork does not.

**Cost.** ~$3.57/sf for 2 in, ~$6.23/sf for 3 in shiplap (Thermacork via Small Planet Supply, Tumwater WA, exclusive Western US distributor). Roughly 2x to 3x rigid foam at the same R-value.

**Workability for an Airstream.** Cork is rigid. It cuts beautifully with a serrated knife or hot wire, but it does not bend around the Airstream's tight curve in thicknesses above ~1 inch. Strategy: cork goes in the flatter wall panels, the floor, and as a continuous ceiling cap; thinner cork (½ in to 1 in) or a more conformable layer goes in the tighter curves at the roof crown and end caps. Adhered with a vapor-permeable construction adhesive (e.g., a hybrid polymer, not solvent-based contact cement) or mechanically fastened against furring strips.

**Why it's the headline material.** Cork is the only insulator on this list that is simultaneously: bio-based, vapor-open, naturally fire-resistant without additives, naturally pest-resistant, durable for 50+ years, and a meaningful acoustic absorber. For a healing-land-adjacent mobile recording studio, that combination is uniquely strong.

#### A2. Sheep wool batts (Havelock, Black Mountain, Oregon Shepherd)
**What it is.** Cleaned, carded, mildly boron-treated sheep wool, formed into batts or sold as loose fill for blowing into cavities.

**Performance.**
- R-value: 3.6 per inch (batt), 4.3 per inch (loose fill)
- Vapor: μ-value of 1, fully vapor-open. Absorbs up to 35% of its weight in water vapor **without losing thermal performance**. This is unique among common insulators. It actively buffers cabin humidity.
- Acoustic: excellent broadband absorber. Crimped fibers create the same matrix that makes rockwool work, but lighter.
- Fire: self-extinguishing. Wool requires sustained external flame; it will not propagate fire on its own.
- Off-gassing: none. Wool is the indoor air quality benchmark.
- Pest: boron treatment handles moths; the lanolin discourages most other pests.
- Bonus: actively scrubs formaldehyde and other VOCs from cabin air. Free air purification for the life of the build.

**Cost.** $1.50 to $2.50/sf depending on source (Havelock direct, Sandy Vans, Sage Restoration). Roughly competitive with rigid foam and noticeably cheaper than cork.

**Workability for an Airstream.** Wool batts conform to curves and irregular cavities far better than cork. Tear by hand, friction-fit, or hold in place with twine/string lacing. **This is the right material for the curved sections cork cannot bend into.** Designed-for-vans batts (Havelock's "Van Insulation" line, 2 in R7) are sized for typical van rib spacing.

**Why we want it.** Wool is the moisture management layer. Two people breathing in a sealed tube produce a lot of water vapor; wool can hold it through a session and release it overnight without supporting mold. Pair with cork or alone, wool earns its place by handling the humidity story.

#### A3. Hemp wool (Hempitecture HempWool)
**What it is.** Hemp fiber batts bonded with a polyester binder, marketed as a vegan alternative to sheep wool.

**Performance.** R-value comparable to sheep wool (~R-3.7/in). Vapor-open, moisture-tolerant, non-toxic, biodegradable. Made from a rapidly renewable crop that sequesters carbon during growth.

**Caveat.** Class E fire rating, which is the lowest tier. In a recording studio with electronics, this matters less than in a residence; in a mobile setting, it is one more reason to ensure your wiring is impeccable.

**Cost.** Similar to wool, ~$1.80 to $2.50/sf.

**When to use it.** As a sheep wool substitute when the build wants to be fully vegan, or as a blended cavity fill alongside cork.

#### A4. Rockwool / mineral wool batts (Rockwool Comfortbatt, Roxul Safe'n'Sound)
**What it is.** Spun basalt rock and slag, formed into dense batts.

**Performance.**
- R-value: 3.7 to 4.0 per inch
- Vapor: fully vapor-open, repels liquid water
- Acoustic: the studio industry's go-to cavity fill. Safe'n'Sound is specifically optimized for acoustic absorption inside walls.
- Fire: non-combustible, melts at over 2000°F. Often used in fire-stopping assemblies.
- Off-gassing: low VOC, but contains a formaldehyde-based binder in most product lines.
- Pest: rodents and insects ignore it.

**Cost.** ~$0.80 to $1.30/sf. Cheap.

**Workability.** Easier to cut than fiberglass, denser, holds its shape. Conforms to curves moderately well; better than cork, worse than wool. Itchy fibers; gloves and an N95 mandatory during install.

**Why it appears in both builds.** Rockwool is the workhorse acoustic fill. Cork or wool can do the work, but rockwool is genuinely good at this job and cheap enough to be the default in the cavities that purely need an absorber rather than a healing-land statement. The Airstream brief already names it for the acoustic plan.

#### A5. 3M Thinsulate SM600L (the van-conversion default)
**What it is.** Polyester and polypropylene microfiber batting, engineered by 3M specifically for vehicles. The product everyone in van-build forums uses.

**Performance.**
- R-value: 3.2 per inch (one of the lowest on this list per inch). 1 in roll gives R-3.2, 1.5 in gives R-4.8.
- Vapor-open and hydrophobic at the same time: vapor passes through, liquid water does not absorb.
- Acoustic: very good. Microfiber friction converts sound energy to heat efficiently.
- Off-gassing: low, automotive-spec.

**Cost.** ~$8 to $10 per linear foot at 60 in wide. Among the more expensive options per square foot.

**Workability.** Lightweight, malleable, easy to cut, designed to be glued to vehicle interiors without sagging across years of vibration.

**Why it shows up.** Honest comparison: Thinsulate is the obvious default and we should know why we'd choose anything else over it. Answer: lower R-per-inch than cork or wool, polyester base, and no humidity buffering. For a recording studio where every dB of acoustic isolation and every degree of thermal performance matters, the natural-fiber options out-perform it on the dimensions we care most about. Thinsulate stays in the library as a comparison anchor.

#### A6. Polyiso rigid board (foil-faced)
**What it is.** Closed-cell polyisocyanurate foam board, foil-faced both sides. The "yellow board" of building supply stores.

**Performance.**
- R-value: 6 to 6.5 per inch new, **drifts down to ~5/in over 20 years** as the blowing agent leaks out
- Vapor: foil facing creates a class I vapor retarder
- Fire: tested but requires a thermal barrier in living spaces
- Off-gassing: moderate during cure, low after
- Pest: rodents will not nest in it

**Cost.** ~$1.20 to $1.80/sf for 1.5 in. Very cheap per R.

**Workability.** Rigid, cuts cleanly, taped seams with aluminum foil tape become a continuous vapor barrier. Does not conform to curves.

**Why it matters.** Polyiso is the strongest argument for itself. If maximum R-per-inch in cavities too shallow for cork or wool is the goal, polyiso is the answer. It also becomes the de facto vapor barrier for the wall. Trade-off: it's a fossil-derived plastic that off-gasses during the first months of life. In the indie build it is optional; in the professional build it appears in the shallowest cavities only.

#### A7. Closed-cell spray polyurethane foam (the dominant industry default)
**What it is.** Two-part isocyanate-and-polyol foam sprayed on-site, expands 30x, cures rigid.

**Performance.** R-6 to R-7 per inch, full air seal, full vapor barrier. Genuinely excellent performance.

**Why both builds avoid it.**
- **Skin damage risk.** Documented to bulge vintage Airstream skins from the inside as it cures (exothermic, expanding pressure on soft aluminum). Patched panels and panels with thin metal are especially vulnerable.
- **Irreversibility.** Adheres to the skin permanently. Any future electrical, plumbing, or skin repair requires chemical or mechanical removal of the foam.
- **Long-term failure mode.** Forum reports of opening old foamed Airstreams to find the foam **turned to powder at the bottom of cavities**, providing zero R-value and concealing rot.
- **Off-gassing.** Isocyanates require respirators during install; cured foam off-gasses for months. Misformulated batches off-gas for years.
- **HFC blowing agents** in most formulations are potent greenhouse gases (1,000x to 3,000x CO₂ equivalent).

It is the right material for fast commercial van builds where reversibility doesn't matter and the installer does this weekly. It is the wrong material for a healing-land-adjacent vintage Airstream we want to still be working in 2046.

#### A8. Aerogel blanket (Aspen Spaceloft)
**What it is.** Silica aerogel suspended in a fiberglass mat. NASA-grade thermal insulation.

**Performance.** R-10.3 per inch. The highest R-per-inch of any insulation in this list by a wide margin. Hydrophobic.

**Cost.** ~$25 to $95 per square foot per inch. **5x to 20x cork.**

**When to use.** Thermal bridges. Specifically: the steel ribs that touch the aluminum skin. A ½ in to 1 in strip of aerogel applied to the inner face of each rib breaks the thermal bridge that otherwise conducts heat straight past the cavity fill. Skin spot temperatures can swing 40 to 50°F with vs. without rib breaks. Used only as a thermal-bridge layer, the total aerogel cost stays manageable (~$200 to $500 for the whole trailer).

Dusty in install; gloves, eyes, dust mask.

### B. Vapor / air control layers

The role here is to control where water vapor goes inside the wall assembly. This is the layer most often misunderstood. In a heated, vapor-producing interior (two people breathing in a sealed tube) against a cold aluminum skin, vapor wants to migrate outward and condense on the first cold surface it reaches. Strategies differ in where they catch that vapor.

#### B1. Smart vapor retarder (CertainTeed MemBrain, Pro Clima Intello)
**What it does.** Membrane that is vapor-tight in winter (low perm) and vapor-open in summer (high perm). Catches condensation risk in cold drives, releases moisture during warm storage.

**Why we want it in both builds.** The Airstream goes through every weather condition. A smart retarder handles seasonal swing better than a fixed poly sheet.

**Cost.** ~$0.30/sf.

#### B2. Aluminum foil tape (1.88 in or 2 in wide, real metal not metallized)
**What it does.** Seals every seam of the foil-faced layers and every penetration. Becomes the actual air barrier of the assembly.

**Cost.** ~$15 to $25 per 50 yd roll. You will use more rolls than you expect.

#### B3. Acoustical sealant (Tremco, OSI Pro-Series)
**What it does.** Non-hardening sealant for every joint where two assembly layers meet. Critical for both vapor control and acoustic isolation; an unsealed gap is both a sound leak and a vapor highway.

#### B4. Plain 6-mil polyethylene sheet
**What it does.** Classic vapor barrier, cheap. **Wrong choice for this build.** In a mobile vehicle with seasonal swings, a fixed-perm poly sheet traps moisture on the wrong side roughly half the year. Listed here only to flag: do not use this.

### C. Decoupling / sound isolation layers

These layers reduce sound transmission by breaking the rigid path that vibration would otherwise travel.

#### C1. Mass loaded vinyl (MLV), 1 lb/sf
**What it is.** Dense, limp, rubber-loaded vinyl sheet. Heavy.

**What it does.** Adds mass to a wall assembly. Sound transmission loss is governed by mass; MLV cheats around the rigidity penalty of just adding more drywall.

**Cost.** ~$1.50 to $2.50/sf at 1 lb density.

**Trade-off in a mobile build.** Heavy. ~360 sf of wall surface at 1 lb/sf adds 360 lb to the trailer. Use selectively (high-priority quiet zones: between cabin and HVAC compressor wall, around the mic position) rather than continuously.

#### C2. Green Glue (visco-elastic damping compound)
**What it does.** Sandwiched between two layers of rigid sheet (typically two layers of ½ in or ⅝ in plywood, MDF, or drywall), converts vibration to negligible heat. Adds 5 to 9 STC points to the same two-layer assembly.

**Cost.** ~$25 per tube; one tube treats ~16 sf.

**Why it's in the professional build.** With cork and wool already in the cavity, Green Glue between the inner finish layer (e.g., 6 mm plywood + acoustic panel face) is the affordable upgrade that takes the room from "very quiet" to "professional quiet."

#### C3. Resilient sound isolation clips + hat channel (RSIC, Whisper Clip)
**What they do.** Float the inner finish wall on rubber-isolated clips so it does not rigidly touch the structural ribs. The single highest-impact decoupling move in studio construction.

**Trade-off in an Airstream.** Standard residential clips and hat channel are designed for ⅝ in drywall on 16 in stud spacing. The Airstream's rib spacing (~6 in to 8 in) and curved geometry mean you'd be specifying a custom or trimmed system. **Professional build only.**

#### C4. U-boats / floor decouplers (Auralex U-boat, Sylomer pads)
**What they do.** Rubber pads under the floor frame so the finish floor floats off the trailer subfloor.

**Trade-off in a mobile vehicle.** Mass loading matters. A floating floor needs precisely the right mass to compress the pads into their working range. In a trailer where the live load (people, gear) varies hugely, the math is harder than in a fixed building. **Professional build only, and only if budget allows working with an acoustician to size correctly.** Skipping this is fine; the trailer-frame-to-axle suspension is already doing some isolation work.

### D. Reflective / radiant barriers

#### D1. Reflectix, Prodex, double-bubble foil
**What they do.** Reflect radiant heat. Useful as an outer-skin facing layer to bounce summer infrared back outward before it loads the cavity.

**Caveat.** Marketing claims of "R-15 equivalent" are mostly fiction; real-world reflective performance requires an adjacent air gap of ¾ in or more, which the Airstream cavity does not provide. As a thin first layer against the skin **with a small air gap maintained by furring**, they contribute meaningfully to summer heat rejection. Without the air gap, they contribute almost nothing.

#### D2. Low-e house wrap (with adjacent air gap)
Performs similarly to Reflectix in the same configuration. Slightly more durable.

### E. Finish layers (the inside face)

These are the layers the room sees and hears. They are part of the insulation story because they affect both vapor handling and acoustic absorption.

#### E1. Acoustic-grade plywood, 6 mm to 9 mm
Lightweight, structural, looks good with a finish oil. Becomes the inner air barrier when seam-taped and sealed.

#### E2. Acoustic panel face (cork tiles, wool felt, hemp felt, GIK Acoustics fabric panels)
The final absorber surface, tuned to the room's frequency response.

#### E3. Wood slat / fabric over batts
Visually warm, acoustically effective. The "Scandinavian sauna" look. Slats are a few inches apart with wool or cork behind the slat gap; the cavity behind continues to absorb.

## Two complete build paths

Both builds share: a thermal-bridge layer (aerogel strips on every steel rib), a smart vapor retarder somewhere in the assembly, every seam sealed with foil tape and acoustical sealant, weep holes at the C-channel kept clear. Both builds avoid: closed-cell spray foam, plain polyethylene sheet, fiberglass batts.

### Indie build: wool-and-cork, volunteer-installable, healthy-first

**Aimed at.** Aiylah, Takata, and the Mystic Hearts credit-pool volunteers. Healthy materials, every layer reversible, no specialty licensing needed for any insulation step (still need a licensed electrician for the wiring run that lives between layers).

**The stack, skin-side outward to room-side:**

1. **Rib thermal break.** ½ in aerogel blanket strips, cut to rib width, adhered to the inner face of every steel rib with a hybrid polymer adhesive. The single most impactful 200 ft of insulation in the whole trailer.

2. **Air gap, then reflective layer.** ¼ in furring strips against the skin between ribs, then a layer of Prodex or low-e wrap stapled to the furring. The air gap is what makes the reflective layer actually work in summer.

3. **Primary cavity fill.**
   - **Flat sections (most of the walls, the side floor lockers):** 2 in expanded cork board, cut to friction-fit between ribs, adhered with vapor-permeable hybrid adhesive at a few contact points (not continuously).
   - **Curved sections (roof crown, end caps, tight rib spacing under windows):** 2 in Havelock sheep wool batts, friction-fit or laced with twine to keep them in place. Wool conforms; cork does not.
   - **Floor:** 2 in cork board over the new subfloor, glued down. Cork's compressive strength handles foot traffic; wool would compress and lose R.

4. **Smart vapor retarder.** CertainTeed MemBrain stretched across the cavity face, every seam taped with acoustical-grade tape, every penetration (wiring, plumbing, mounting points) sealed.

5. **Finish layer.** 9 mm acoustic-grade plywood, screwed to furring against the ribs, seams caulked with acoustical sealant. Oiled or left raw for the warm-wood interior.

6. **Acoustic face.** Cork tiles or wool-felt acoustic panels in the priority listening zones (around the mic position, behind the host, on the ceiling immediately above the table). The rest of the room gets the bare plywood look.

**Approximate R-value at the wall:** R-9 to R-11 (aerogel rib break + cork or wool cavity fill at 2 in + foiled vapor side). Approximate weight: ~350 to 400 lb of insulation total.

**Approximate cost of insulation materials only** (not labor, not electrical, not HVAC):
- Aerogel (rib breaks, ~80 sf at 0.5 in): ~$400
- Reflective wrap and furring: ~$200
- Cork (~180 sf at 2 in): ~$650
- Wool batts (~120 sf at 2 in): ~$250
- Smart vapor retarder (~360 sf): ~$110
- Foil tape, acoustical sealant, fasteners: ~$200
- Inner plywood finish layer: ~$400
- Acoustic face panels (priority zones): ~$300

**Indie total: ~$2,500 in insulation materials**, plus labor in Mystic Hearts credits.

**Time estimate.** 80 to 140 volunteer hours total across all insulation phases, spread over four to six weekend work parties.

**What this build is good at.** Healthy interior, reversible, repairable by any volunteer with hand tools, ages well, looks like a place you'd want to record a podcast about somatic practice in. Acoustic isolation good enough for "well-recorded podcast" tier.

**What it's not.** Not a sealed broadcast studio. Loud truck passing within 30 ft will be audible at the mic. Acceptable for the use case; not acceptable for an album mix room.

### Professional build: layered isolation, acoustician-tuned, max performance

**Aimed at.** A future tier where the studio is doing paid commercial recording for outside clients and needs to compete with brick-and-mortar studios on noise floor. Sourced through a licensed RV restoration shop with an acoustician consult.

**The stack, skin-side outward to room-side:**

1. **Rib thermal break.** 1 in aerogel blanket (double the indie spec) on every rib.

2. **Air gap, then continuous reflective layer.** Same as indie, but Prodex with sealed seams.

3. **Primary cavity fill, layered.**
   - Inner half of cavity: 1 in polyiso foil-faced rigid board, cut to fit between ribs, seams foil-taped. This is the vapor and air control layer doing double duty as thermal.
   - Outer half of cavity (toward the room): 1 in expanded cork board (flat) or 1 in Havelock wool batt (curved), friction-fit against the polyiso. Cork's vapor-openness and acoustic absorption sit on the warm side where they're useful; polyiso's vapor-closure sits on the cold side where it stops the vapor drive.
   - Floor: 2 in cork over subfloor, plus a top layer of 6 mm cork underlayment under the finish floor for impact isolation.

4. **Smart vapor retarder + foil tape.** Same as indie, doubled up at penetrations.

5. **Decoupling layer.** Trimmed-down resilient sound isolation clips (RSIC) on every rib, hat channel running vertically, holds the inner wall off the rib structure entirely. **This is the single biggest acoustic improvement over the indie build.**

6. **Mass loaded vinyl.** 1 lb/sf MLV stapled to the hat channel, every seam overlapped 2 in and sealed. Adds mass to the inner wall where it does the most isolation work.

7. **Inner wall: two layers.**
   - 6 mm plywood
   - Green Glue (full coverage, ~16 sf per tube)
   - 9 mm acoustic plywood

8. **Acoustic face.** Tuned by an acoustician based on REW measurements. Cork tiles, wool panels, hemp felt, slatted absorbers in proportions specified per frequency response of the actual room. Some zones get bass traps in the corners (rockwool wrapped in fabric).

9. **Floor finish.** Floating floor on U-boats or Sylomer pads, sized for the calculated live load of two people plus gear. Optional; skip if budget tight or live load too variable.

**Approximate R-value at the wall:** R-13 to R-16 (aerogel + polyiso + cork/wool + decoupled inner wall thermal contribution). Approximate weight: ~600 to 750 lb of insulation and decoupling combined. The trailer's axle and tire ratings need to be checked against finished total weight.

**Approximate cost of insulation and decoupling materials only:**
- Aerogel (rib breaks at 1 in): ~$800
- Polyiso (~360 sf at 1 in): ~$540
- Cork (~180 sf at 1 in): ~$350
- Wool batts (~120 sf at 1 in): ~$150
- Smart vapor retarder: ~$110
- Foil tape, sealant: ~$300
- RSIC clips + hat channel: ~$650
- MLV (~360 sf at 1 lb): ~$700
- Inner wall plywood (two layers): ~$800
- Green Glue (~25 tubes): ~$625
- Acoustician tuning (acoustic face + corner traps): ~$1,500
- Floor decoupling (U-boats or Sylomer): ~$400

**Professional total: ~$6,900 in materials**, plus ~$15,000 to $25,000 in labor at restoration-shop rates ($150 to $250/hr) plus ~$1,500 to $3,000 for the acoustician consult.

**Time estimate.** 200 to 350 professional hours over three to five months.

**What this build is good at.** Real recording studio acoustics in a 27 ft trailer. STC in the high 40s to mid 50s achievable. Tracks vocals for commercial release. Tracks instruments with a real noise floor.

**What it's not.** Not volunteer-buildable from end to end; the decoupling layer, MLV, and tuned acoustic face need craftspeople who've done this before. The indie build can be the first version; the professional layers can be added in a later phase if the studio's revenue justifies it. Not a one-day decision.

## A blended path (optional)

If the realistic answer is "indie now, professional possible later," the indie build is structured so that the professional decoupling layers (RSIC clips, hat channel, MLV, Green Glue inner wall) can be added on top of the existing assembly without ripping anything out. The wool, cork, and smart vapor retarder all stay; the new layers go room-side of them. This is the recommended path: build indie, measure with REW, decide later whether the room needs the upgrade.

The one decision that does need to be made up front: **rib thermal breaks (aerogel) and the air gap behind the reflective layer**. Those are inside-the-cavity layers. Adding them later means opening the wall. Spend the ~$600 to do this in the indie build even if nothing else gets upgraded.

## Open decisions for koH

1. **Cork sourcing.** Western US is Small Planet Supply (Tumwater WA). Lead time for full-trailer quantity is 4 to 8 weeks; order before demo finishes.
2. **Wool batt vendor.** Havelock direct vs. Sandy Vans (van-cut sizes) vs. Sage Restoration. Sandy Vans is likely the right choice for the curved sections (pre-sized for van rib spacing).
3. **Window strategy.** Insulation choice interacts with the window decision in the Airstream brief. Dual-pane replacement reduces the insulation load; original-plus-secondary-glazing keeps the vintage look but loads the wall assembly with more isolation work. Decide together.
4. **Rib thermal break commitment.** $400 to $800 in aerogel that 95% of van builds skip. This research's strongest single recommendation.
5. **Decouple or not.** The professional-build decoupling is the place the build can grow over time. Indie first, measure, decide.
6. **Whether to spec Mass Loaded Vinyl in priority zones only.** Could be a middle path: skip the continuous MLV of the pro build, but apply MLV behind the host position and behind the wall closest to the HVAC compressor. ~80 sf instead of 360 sf, ~$160 instead of $700, much of the perceptual benefit.
